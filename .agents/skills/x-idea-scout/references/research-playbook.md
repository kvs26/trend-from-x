# Research playbook

## Find conversations that can lead to a product

Use the invocation's chosen direction (useful tools, playful experiences, or both) to guide searches and shortlisting. Do not automatically favor utilities over playful products when the user chose both. Use a manageable mix of searches rather than mechanically executing a query catalog:

- Requests and dissatisfaction: phrases such as "wish there was", "someone should build", "why is there no", or a specific expensive/manual task.
- Timely conversations: new capabilities, launches, changing workflows, confusing choices, or a topic that generates many concrete questions.
- Workarounds: people maintaining spreadsheets, repeatedly copying information, collecting links, or doing a task by hand.
- Shareable experiences: people comparing results, displaying an identity or achievement, inviting others, or circulating a useful resource.
- Distribution openings: a specific request or discussion where a small working product would make a relevant contribution.

Start with recent conversations, often the last week, and broaden when the topic needs context. Inspect both recent posts and stronger engagement where useful. Read replies to the most promising signals; search for independent accounts with the same need. Reposts and repeated promotional text do not constitute independent demand.

Follow visible browser state and the available tool documentation. Adapt queries to what the page actually supports. Avoid blind query grids, exhaustive timeline collection, or treating suggested search phrases as a required sequence. If a search family is unproductive, move on. End when the strongest candidates have enough evidence to make a useful decision or further searching is unlikely to resolve the key uncertainty.

## Record what was actually observed

For promising evidence capture:

- Direct post URL, author/account, post date, and observation date (with timezone when time-sensitive).
- A short paraphrase of the request or behavior; distinguish the author's assertion from independent verification.
- Visible replies, likes, reposts, views, or bookmarks where useful. Label missing counts as unavailable. Counts are a snapshot, not an estimate of future performance.
- Relevant reply themes, independent corroboration, workarounds, explicit purchase intent, and counterevidence. Note when replies or the full post could not be read.
- Whether the post is promotional, sponsored, a launch announcement, or an actual request; whether the proposed solution already exists.

Search snippets are leads. Open the original evidence when possible before relying on it. Never manufacture posts, counts, customer quotes, or access. One popular post is a lead; do not call it a sustained trend without corroboration over time or multiple independent signals. A low-engagement but concrete paid problem can be stronger than a large vague conversation.

## Compare the opportunity

Use qualitative judgments (strong, mixed, weak, or unknown) with short reasons. Avoid numerical scores that imply more certainty than the evidence supports. Compare:

| Dimension | What matters |
| --- | --- |
| Demand | A specific user and task, frequency or intensity of need, independent signals, existing workarounds, willingness to pay |
| Timing | Why this is relevant now, launch window, evidence of persistence or rapid decay |
| Sharing | A concrete output/action worth sharing, the sharer's motivation, recipient value, path to more users |
| Distribution | Reachable conversations and communities, first-user plan, dependence on a single person's response |
| Buildability | Small complete user journey, data availability, integrations, external permissions, ongoing support and maintenance |
| Economics | Who pays and why, suitable pricing model, likely variable costs, launch costs, payment friction |
| Differentiation | Existing substitutes, a narrow reason to switch/use this, any plausible accumulating advantage |

Prioritize sharing, reachable users, and fast testing for this founder, while checking whether the money mechanism is plausible. A durable moat is useful but not an admission requirement for a small experiment. Avoid equating complex infrastructure or expensive data access with a competitive advantage by itself.

Check the most relevant alternatives using current official pages, pricing, and docs. Identify a specific gap or audience rather than claiming there is no competition. Confirm material API/data dependencies before recommending a build; label unverified costs and access requirements. Do not assume browser-readable X content creates a free production API for a proposed product.

For every shortlisted idea, specify who pays, what they receive, a tentative price or pricing basis, the likely route to the first payment, and the costs that affect profit. Do not count "ads later" or "sponsorship" as a complete earning method: state the audience or traffic prerequisite and how advertisers or sponsors would be reached. Acknowledge when the earning method is weak instead of using virality to hide it.

For a playful experience, explain why anyone pays, sponsors, or returns after sharing. For a utility, explain how sharing happens naturally without exposing private user information. For directories and marketplaces, account for sourcing data and acquiring the first participants; a simple interface does not remove the cold-start problem. Fit the first usable release into a week; reduce scope or explain why a candidate misses the founder's launch target.

## Turn evidence into a test

For the recommended idea, specify:

- One target user, one promise, and the smallest complete experience; list the main features to leave out.
- A plausible sharing loop: trigger → shareable artifact/action → recipient value → product visit → further participation.
- A concrete route to the first users, including a relevant X thread if observed and a fallback independent of an influencer responding.
- A revenue hypothesis appropriate to the product: one-time purchase, subscription, sponsorship, paid placement, or another explicit model. Do not force recurring billing on one-use value.
- Transparent revenue arithmetic toward at least USD 5,000 cumulative revenue over six months, including payer count and price. For example, 100 purchases at USD 50 produces USD 5,000 gross revenue before costs and fees; that is arithmetic, not a demand forecast. Recurring-revenue scenarios must account for when customers join and how long they stay. Show estimated costs and possible profit separately, stating which costs or fees remain unknown.
- Rough effort, launch cost, recurring cost, usage costs, operational burden, and important unknowns. Distinguish revenue from profit.
- The cheapest next test, the user's outreach tasks, a suitable observation window, and continue/change/stop thresholds tied to meaningful behavior such as activation, sharing, return use, or payment. Choose thresholds per idea rather than universal quotas.

Keep facts, interpretation, and proposed tests distinct. If evidence is thin, recommend a reversible test or a watch status rather than a confident build recommendation.
