# Playful brand promotion — second research round

Date: 2026-11-05, Asia/Kolkata. Sources checked during this session, around 15:15–15:45 IST. Direction: playful experiences that promote a brand, creator, or community. This continues the first scouting run; no new category choice is required. No product has been selected for building.

## Main finding

The broad direction has credible precedents. The stronger commercial proposition connects the interaction to a buyer's goal: a launch people discuss, a helpful product recommendation, a gift sent to a friend, an event interaction, or a tracked offer. A logo on a game by itself does not explain why the buyer should pay or renew.

For this founder's interest in sharing, investigate **P004: small interactive launch rooms** first, alongside **P005: playful product-match cards** for a clearer connection to purchases. P006 is an emotionally shareable alternative; P007 has a more operational sales case and less natural online sharing. These rankings remain provisional while buyer and business-model preferences are discussed.

PinchPop can be one possible interaction inside a campaign. Its camera mechanic should not determine the business model before customer conversations. Keep the original P001 evidence and assumptions in the first report.

## Commercial precedents and competitive checks

### 1. Marketing games are bought for specific outcomes

[Playable's Virgin Red case](https://playable.com/cases/virgin-red/) describes repeated branded games supporting app engagement and sign-ups. The vendor reports 131,000 competition entries and 3,500 new members for one campaign. Prizes, partners, and an established audience were involved; the results do not predict a small founder's performance.

[Playable's Carlsberg case](https://playable.com/cases/carlsberg/) describes a purchase-code campaign and reports twice the redemption rate of its comparator campaigns. The lesson here is measurable customer action and repeatable campaign setup. Its purchase/prize mechanics are not part of our proposed first MVP.

[Drimlike's Coty retail campaign](https://www.drimlike.com/en/blog/marketing-games-boost-in-store-traffic.html) used a short quiz, QR access, staff invitations, and existing airport channels. It reports over 1,000 promotional codes distributed. Code distribution is not the same as code redemption or incremental purchases. This is also evidence that a distribution plan matters.

### 2. A playful recommendation can connect to a product purchase

[Interact's Henry's House of Coffee case](https://www.tryinteract.com/blog/henrys-house-of-coffee-converts-9-of-quiz-takers-into-customers/) describes taste questions, optional email signup, and links to relevant Shopify collections. The article was updated May 18, 2026 and refers to an owner email from 2021. Its headline says 9% of quiz takers become customers, while the quoted owner labels 9% as leads-to-purchase. The denominator is ambiguous; do not use it as a verified quiz-to-sale benchmark.

[Interact's Atlassian case](https://www.tryinteract.com/blog/how-atlassian-uses-quizzes-to-create-differentiated-content/) describes meeting-decision, leadership-style, and assessment quizzes used as interactive content. It supports a broader B2B audience beyond consumer retail, without establishing a revenue effect for our idea.

### 3. Sharing can be part of the experience itself

[VCCP's Cadbury Worldwide Hide case](https://www.vccp.com/work/cadbury/the-worldwide-hide-is-back/), dated February 27, 2024, describes hiding a virtual egg and sending a personal clue to a loved one, with an optional physical gift purchase. The interaction inherently involves another person. It had a substantial campaign and established brand behind it; a simple first release should not reproduce mapping and fulfillment infrastructure.

[Dot.vu's Grasværk greeting case](https://dot.vu/client-success-stories/personalized-birthday-video-greeting) describes fans creating and sharing greetings featuring a TV character. It reports qualitative brand value but no numerical purchase result. A smaller adaptation could use an original/authorized mascot and lightweight 2D personalization rather than video generation.

### 4. Agency delivery exists, and competition is inexpensive

[Gamify's agency page](https://www.gamify.com/gamification-agency) describes working behind creative agencies as a branded-game delivery partner. [Drimify's reseller documentation](https://help.drimify.com/en/article/resellers-and-referrals-14i1s55/) also describes client-branded resale. These establish a channel worth investigating; they do not establish demand for our services.

[Adgamify's agency page](https://www.adgamify.com/pages/agencies) publicly lists white-label plans from USD 24.99 per client/month, including games and campaign features. Its example client names and lead counts are explicitly illustrative; no use is made of them as customer results. This undercuts a generic claim that adding a logo to a basic game is a unique product.

[Interact's pricing page](https://www.tryinteract.com/plans/) displayed Lite at USD 27/month and white-labeled Growth at USD 53/month, both billed annually. These are subscription equivalents, not month-to-month or one-off purchase prices. A bespoke offer needs value in creative direction, an audience-specific result, or delivery rather than simply reproducing a quiz editor.

[Playable's pricing](https://playable.com/pricing/) requires a sales conversation; no amount was publicly verified. [Drimify's current plan help page](https://help.drimify.com/en/article/drimify-pricing-plans-28sh6x/) describes participation-based tiers and white labeling, but the paid pricing page returned 403, so no paid amount was adopted.

[Dot.vu's February 10, 2026 announcement](https://news.dot.vu/press-release/ai-interactive-builder-launch/) describes generating interactive experiences with AI. Treat this as an announced competitor capability, not an independently tested performance claim. Agentic production by itself is unlikely to distinguish our offer.

### P004 — Small interactive launch rooms

**Simple example:** A musician releasing a song gets a playful desktop or illustrated room. Fans explore three objects, uncover a preview, and share a discovery card and link. The musician's own release supplies the audience's reason to care. For a product brand, the discovery could reveal a new flavor or design.

**Sharing loop:** Existing fans arrive from the creator → find a detail or reveal → post a screenshot/card → other fans explore → they follow the creator's release link. Avoid assuming a puzzle automatically causes sharing. Include a clear direct path to the release so exploration does not obstruct interested visitors.

**What the buyer purchases:** Creative setup using their approved assets, one compact interactive page, one revision, 30-day hosting, and anonymous event reporting such as starts, reveals, shares clicked, and outbound release clicks. These clicks do not establish downstream purchases. Initially a templated service; no full drag-and-drop builder or campaign SaaS.

**Why it could be different:** A narrow, enjoyable launch format and fast delivery for one creator segment. The TXT observations are relevant inspiration, but large-artist results cannot justify a price for independent artists. Small agencies may be a better paying channel than individual creators; that needs conversations.

**Keep the first release small:** One theme, three authored interactions, one reveal, one share card, one destination. Exclude 3D worlds, live chat, user uploads, AI-generated media during play, hidden copyrighted assets, and a general room editor. All content comes from the client or is original/licensed.

**Money and cost scenario:** 15 campaigns × USD 349 = USD 5,235 gross over six months. Planning envelope: USD 20–100 initial infrastructure/assets, USD 0–25/month for modest-volume hosting, and 2–4 hours of work per reskin/revision package once the template exists. Actual traffic, provider terms, support, payment fees, and asset needs could change this. No customer count is forecast.

**Cheapest next evidence:** The founder talks to five creators with an upcoming release or agencies that serve them. Show two simple concept boards and ask which existing release activity this would replace or improve, who controls that budget, and what result matters. One paid pilot or firm scoped purchase discussion would be stronger than praise. Recommend a prototype only after selecting this candidate.

### P005 — Shareable taste cards with useful matches

**Simple example:** A coffee shop's five-question experience gives a playful identity such as a cozy-night coffee explorer, a shareable illustrated card, and two coffees already sold by that shop. A friend follows the shared card to discover their own match.

**Buyer value:** A clearer path from curiosity to suitable products. The shop supplies product facts and mapping; the experience should not pretend that playful personality labels are scientific assessments. The Interact case supports recommendation use, but does not establish our conversion rate.

**Offer:** USD 299 for one audience-specific quiz, up to a small agreed set of result types, matching rules, client-approved content, cards, existing product links, and a report. Initially no mandatory email capture or CRM integration; those add scope and should follow a real buyer need. Test the result before asking the user to subscribe. A repeat campaign or update fee is optional future work, not assumed recurring revenue.

**One-week fit and economics:** One category, 5–7 questions, 4–6 results, and a small catalog. Estimate 18–26 hours; USD 20–100 initial and USD 0–25/month at modest usage. Human content approval may be the bottleneck. Seventeen packages × USD 299 = USD 5,083 gross. Per-client content work can eliminate margin if revisions are unbounded.

**Main test:** Ask five merchants with existing site/social traffic whether product choice is a real problem and compare our offer with using Interact directly. Track completion, product clicks, voluntary sharing, and downstream checkout data only if the merchant can provide a suitable measurement method. Product clicks are not paid orders. Status: investigate for commercial clarity, with moderate confidence in category demand and low confidence in our offer's differentiation.

### P006 — Interactive gifts people pass to someone else

**Simple example:** A chocolate or gift brand lets a visitor decorate a small digital gift and write/select a short message. The recipient opens a link, gets an enjoyable reveal, and can create one for another friend. A product link can accompany the experience without obstructing the greeting.

**Why sharing fits:** A greeting already has a recipient, so sharing is part of the task. The Cadbury and Grasværk precedents support this pattern. They do not establish that our implementation will drive purchases or spread beyond existing audiences.

**Offer and buyer:** USD 249 for one themed campaign for a gift shop, creator, or seasonal brand, one revision, and 30-day hosting. The buyer supplies the initial distribution. A brand can pay for the experience itself; sponsorship of an audience we do not yet have is a separate, unvalidated model.

**Small version:** One 2D reveal, a few decorative choices, short controlled personalization, a shareable link/card, and an existing shop destination. No generated celebrity messages, uploaded personal photos, physical shipping, public message gallery, or 3D map. Estimate 20–30 hours, USD 20–100 setup, USD 0–25/month at modest usage; storing messages would add operational requirements.

**Goal arithmetic and test:** 21 campaigns × USD 249 = USD 5,229 gross. Compare the offer to free digital greetings, ordinary social posts, and the client's existing tools. Ask five gift-oriented brands about upcoming seasonal plans, and test whether users actually send and open the experience. Measure recipient opens and onward creation; sales claims require separate order evidence. Status: investigate for sharing, with a weaker immediate ROI case.

### P007 — A small QR challenge for a real occasion

**Simple example:** At a coffee pop-up, a visitor scans a QR code, completes a short flavor challenge, and sees a shop-provided offer. The shop wants more conversations and measurable use of its offer. The discount is the shop's cost and decision, not our revenue.

**Evidence and distinctions:** The Coty case shows retail distribution; [Enorness's TAMKO case](https://enorness.com/case-studies/trade-show-guessing-game) reports a trade-show guessing activity with 228 submissions. Neither establishes a budget or customer request for our proposed small campaign. The elaborate multi-screen version is beyond our first scope.

**Offer:** USD 499 per small event/campaign for the experience, QR asset, one theme, and agreed activity reporting. First version has predefined answers and no public free-text leaderboard or on-site hardware. Any offer uses client-configured codes and terms; [Drimify's code documentation](https://help.drimify.com/en/article/distributing-promo-codes-through-your-app-12ovcau/) illustrates why issuing an on-screen code and redeeming it at checkout are separate steps.

**Costs and goal arithmetic:** Estimate 20–30 hours for the limited first version, USD 20–150 initial setup, USD 0–40/month for modest traffic, plus potentially significant event support. Eleven campaigns × USD 499 = USD 5,489 gross. Travel, hardware, CRM/POS integration, prize administration, and urgent support are outside that estimate. Status: watch unless the founder prefers event/agency sales; relatively easy to name a buyer, less aligned with organic online virality.

## How to judge the earning model

The core sales question is what result a client hopes to improve and whether our fee plus their campaign costs is worthwhile. As an arithmetic illustration only, a USD 300 campaign that produces 30 additional purchases with USD 15 contribution margin each produces USD 450 of contribution before the campaign fee, leaving USD 150. That does not predict 30 sales, and attributing extra purchases requires an appropriate comparison. Discount costs must already be included in the margin assumption.

Do not equate impressions, plays, form submissions, coupon issues, coupon redemptions, and purchases. Select the buyer's main metric before building. If the client has no audience, budget for acquiring it or use an existing community/event; do not promise that a microsite supplies free traffic.

All four first offers include some service work. A self-service editor would require onboarding, content entry, billing, account management, support, and a reason to keep subscribing; it is a later product decision. Pure sponsorship of our own free site requires first establishing an audience sponsors value. Neither model is proven by the proposed campaign prices.

For each offer, positive cash margin is plausible only if paid acquisition, asset costs, hosting, payment fees, and human setup/revision time are controlled. No profit forecast is possible yet. The six-month customer counts above are transparent targets for discussion, not probability-weighted forecasts.

## Subsequent founder discussion — model and growth questions

After the report, the founder tentatively favored creators/communities and small brands/businesses, but said they are new to these models and need concrete examples before choosing. They emphasized an experience with a compelling hook, competition among friends, and organic sharing, with brands or sponsors once usage is meaningful. This suggests exploring an audience-first public product alongside the client-paid offers; it is not a final business-model or product decision.

Keep these three models distinct:

1. **Customized client campaigns:** A business pays for an experience and brings its existing audience. Early income can come from a small number of clients; delivery and sales work recur.
2. **Self-service software:** Businesses pay for a tool to create their own experiences and bring their audiences. Requires useful creation tools, onboarding, billing, and support; a fuller product than a single game.
3. **An audience-first public experience:** We operate the free game and acquire players. Later a sponsor pays for relevant exposure or an optional premium feature adds another income source. This aligns with the sharing question but introduces audience-acquisition and monetization uncertainty.

For the third model, investigate a single 30–60-second browser challenge with a clear demonstration, comparable rounds, a personal challenge link, and a spoiler-free result card. A daily common challenge can support return visits and comparison; neither actual sharing nor retention has been established for our product. Start with an identifiable interest group rather than an assumed global audience on day one. The intended sequence is initial distribution by the founder → completed play → voluntary challenge sharing → recipient play → return use.

Measure game completions, actual recipient plays attributable to challenge links, and later return visits. Share-button clicks are not equivalent to shares or new players. Hypothetically, 100 initial players who bring 30 additional players create a useful 30% referral increment; that rate alone does not sustain growth without further acquisition. These numbers illustrate mechanics and are not benchmarks or forecasts.

An example of the sponsorship format is [Zany Games' public offer](https://zanygames.net/sponsor?offer=daily_challenge), checked 2026-11-05: a labeled daily-challenge sponsorship is offered at USD 99 for a 30-day initial trial. It is a listed offer, not verified sales, revenue, or a suitable rate for us. Our eventual sponsor proposition would depend on measured audience fit, usage, retention, and the sponsor's goal. [TimeGuessr's official site](https://timeguessr.com/) also visibly separates daily and multiplayer play; that is a format precedent, not proof of a growth mechanism or sponsorship revenue.

Next discussion should clarify whether the founder wants to prioritize owning the player audience despite delayed revenue. Do not write a portable build brief, select a candidate, or update the root directions file solely from these exploratory answers.
