The client supplies the initial audience through its social account, mailing list, website, or event. We earn a project fee. ## The focused offer worth testing

**Working hypothesis:** “We turn your approved campaign assets into one short, mobile-friendly browser challenge, with a score/challenge link and a clear next action.” Initially deliver it manually for a project fee. No customer-facing editor or subscription platform is required.

**Simple example:** a small event agency has a coffee-brand pop-up next month. The brand already has a mascot, product pictures, and a promotion. We make a 30-second catching or stacking challenge using those assets. Visitors enter through the agency's QR code or the brand's posts. After playing, they see their score, can challenge a friend, and can visit the existing product or event page. The agency pays us for the agreed campaign deliverable. We do not need a large audience of our own before that sale.

The agency is only one candidate buyer. A small brand could purchase directly. Creators remain possible, but this run supplied weaker evidence for their budgets. Ask which route the founder wants to test before treating agencies as the permanent customer segment.

### Scope and one-week feasibility

Estimate **20–30 hours for the first compact version**, including setup, browser/device checks, deployment, and a revision. This is a planning hypothesis, not a quote. The clock depends on receiving usable assets and approval. Subsequent adaptations might take 4–8 hours if the mechanic and reporting truly remain the same; measure this rather than assuming near-zero fulfillment.

Limit it to one 2D mechanic, one branded theme, one score/result screen, a challenge link, one destination CTA, and basic anonymous campaign events. Exclude custom 3D, live multiplayer, an editable game builder, payment systems, POS/CRM integrations, prizes, camera tracking, on-site hardware, and guaranteed sales. A public leaderboard needs separate scope for score integrity and moderation; a friend challenge can be tested first.

### Distribution and sharing

Initial distribution must be agreed before production: the client's scheduled post/email, website placement, community, or event QR. A campaign with no audience still has an acquisition problem.

Proposed loop: client sends players → someone finishes a short round → challenges a particular friend with a score/link → friend understands the challenge immediately and plays → friend can send a new challenge. The player shares to compete, not to advertise the client. This is a hypothesis. Test recipient play attributable to challenge links, rather than calling share-button clicks “viral growth.” An event game can be commercially useful even if this loop is weak.

### Money and profit assumptions

A tentative first package is **USD 349** for one agreed campaign, one revision, and a bounded hosting period/traffic allowance. That price is unvalidated. Client-supplied assets and tight scope matter; bespoke art, integrations, or event support would change the quote.

Fifteen projects × USD 349 = **USD 5,235 gross revenue** over six months. This is target arithmetic, not a forecast of fifteen buyers. With illustrative assumptions of 5% for payment/refund costs, USD 100 setup, USD 180 shared hosting, and USD 20 extra assets/tools per project, about **USD 4,393** remains before labor, taxes, and unknown costs. These are budget placeholders, not verified vendor quotes or authorized spend.

If the first version takes 30 hours and fourteen adaptations take six each, delivery consumes 114 hours. Valuing that at USD 20/hour leaves about USD 2,113 before sales time and tax. If each job instead takes 20 hours, the same valuation makes the scenario loss-making. This is why we should test delivery effort and paid demand before promising low-cost customization. Traffic, revisions, support, and acquisition can all reduce the margin.

## Next validation: a sale-shaped conversation before a build

The remaining uncertainty is a customer's willingness to purchase our offer, rather than another missing trend search. The founder handles conversations; agents can draft and prepare materials. No outreach is authorized or sent by this report.

1. Pick one initial route for discussion: small agencies with campaign briefs, or small brands with an upcoming launch/event. Use existing public business channels; the rejected X leads are not a qualified prospect list.
2. In five qualified conversations, ask about the last comparable campaign, what they paid or used instead, the next real occasion, who approves spending, supplied assets, audience/distribution, expected outcome, and turnaround. Avoid asking only whether a game sounds cool.
3. Show one simple storyboard/mock concept matched to an actual brief. Ask whether the customer will buy that scope at an explicit fee, with approval milestones and a deposit or agreed payment process. Praise and “send a link when ready” do not count as a purchase.
4. Suggested checkpoint after about two weeks: continue toward a portable brief if one buyer makes a concrete paid commitment and the work fits the time/cost limit. If conversations reveal a need but reject this scope or price, revise the offer. If none can name a funded occasion, stop this offer and change the segment. Five conversations are an initial filter, not a statistical market estimate.

For any pilot, define the client's primary metric before building: completed plays, qualified event conversations, attributable destination visits, or another agreed action. Purchase claims require order evidence and an appropriate comparison. Separately observe completions, recipient plays, client satisfaction, actual delivery hours, and willingness to buy again. No universal conversion or virality benchmark is assumed.

Only after a concrete choice should we create a portable brief for a **new project folder/session**. This folder remains for scouting. Implementation tools, REA, Matt Pocock's workflows, and the actual build harness belong in that later project.
